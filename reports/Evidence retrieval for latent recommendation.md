# Stop selecting evidence, start marginalizing it

The literature points to one answer. An R3-style latent recommender gets the right evidence by changing how evidence is scored and combined, not by building one more retriever over the same user history. Two levers are best supported. First, **marginalize or aggregate over several pieces of evidence instead of making a hard choice**, because hard selection keeps failing even when oracle headroom is large. Second, **train the evidence prior against the frozen decoder's target-conditioned posterior over the *whole* (small) corpus**, Hindsight/Atlas-PDist style, rather than against "which cluster contains the target". Your numbers follow a pattern the literature has already documented. Coverage outruns selection (oracle best-path HR@10 0.2275 vs R3 0.1450). Generator likelihood is a bad selector (7.3% vs 13% chance). Label-trained verifiers deliver little (VRec's own gain on Instruments, the dataset closest to yours, is only about +5–7%). Per-request routers fall below the best single model even when the oracle is far ahead. The less comfortable finding: if every retriever built on the same history fails on the same rows, then reweighting, boosting or verifying that history adds nothing. The only published per-group gains on hard rows come from **new inputs**, mainly item text and LLM-embedded neighbour users. The best-supported next steps, in order: inference-time marginalization over the top-m evidence; a PMI-corrected selector; decoder-posterior distillation over all clusters; group-constrained item decoding to turn correct coarse groups into hits; and a coverage audit plus a test of text-based evidence on R3's misses. Several of the mechanisms below are cited from the research notes' prior knowledge rather than re-checked sources. They are marked **(unverified, from memory)** wherever they appear.

## Your bottleneck is a known pattern, and VRec's smallest gain is on your dataset

Two families of LLM recommenders retrieve evidence from beyond the user's own history. The first retrieves **other users' histories as text**. SCoRe trains a text retriever with SASRec-neighbour supervision, then reranks retrieved users with a "self-assessing" reranker trained by InfoNCE against an LLM-written description of what would help. It injects only the top 1–2 users into a Llama-3.1-8B prompt and reaches Amazon-Games AUC/UAUC **0.6933/0.6353 vs LLaRA 0.6346/0.6035** ([arXiv 2505.19464](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19464)). The second family injects **group- or neighbourhood-level collaborative priors into a latent**, and this is the one that maps onto a single thought token. VRec is built on a pretrained LatentR3 with Qwen2.5-1.5B, which is your exact stack. Per-aspect MLP verifiers predict the target's k-means group (CF/title clusters with k ∈ {15, 20, 25}, plus categories), and the predicted class's weight column is mixed into the reasoning vector as a prototype ([arXiv 2603.07725](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07725v1)). ManCAR, a small non-LLM Transformer, uses a Swing co-interaction graph to build a rank-based teacher distribution over the target and its graph neighbours, then KL-constrains every latent step toward it. Removing that teacher drops CDs NDCG@10 from **0.0282 to 0.0212**, so most of the gain comes from the CF-neighbourhood supervision ([arXiv 2602.20093](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20093)). IntuRec initialises latent reasoning from an encoding of the recommender's own top-K candidate list. The details (peak at K≈15, about one reasoning step) come from a secondary summary and are **unverified** ([arXiv 2606.27684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27684)). RRCM trains a GRPO policy with an outcome-only ranking reward to decide *whether* and *which* memory to retrieve. Only its abstract was read, so no numbers are available ([arXiv 2605.07129](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07129v2)).

Across these papers, the gains come from **externally selected collaborative information, not from more self-computation**, and the effort goes into the selector. SCoRe gets *worse* as the number of retrieved users grows, its best setting keeps only 1–2 users, and its variants without the reranker underperform ([arXiv 2505.19464](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19464)). LatentR3 itself does no analysis of whether the latent is used. On Toys, SFT plus the extra attention layer already reaches **H@5 0.0804 of the full model's 0.0821** ([arXiv 2505.19092](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19092v1)). ReaRec documents latent degradation directly: without regularisation, successive reasoning states reach cosine similarity **0.98–1.00**, and naive multi-step reasoning underperforms the base model ([arXiv 2503.22675](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.22675v3)). Related work finds that LLM layers progressively weaken injected collaborative signals (FreLLM4Rec, [arXiv 2508.10312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10312)). In SCoRe's attention case study, soft CF tokens get little attention while natural-language CF evidence is used ([arXiv 2505.19464](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19464)). Your finding that the decoder converts correct cluster evidence into a hit about 63% of the time therefore matters. It shows that the decoder side of p(y|x,e) works, which no published latent recommender has shown with a counterfactual probe.

VRec is the most important calibration point for your negative result. Its gain over LatentR3 is **+20% R@5 on CDs (0.0963 vs 0.0801) but only about +7% on Instruments (0.1098 vs 0.1022)** ([arXiv 2603.07725](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07725v1)). The notes record a smaller figure of about +5% at R@10 for Instruments, from a separate reading. VRec reports no sweep over the number of groups k, no accuracy for the verifier's group prediction, and no long-tail breakdown. One reading of its update rule (c_i = 1/f_i, with f the verifier entropy) appears internally inconsistent about sign, and this is **unresolved** ([arXiv 2603.07725](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07725v1)). Your 20-group VRec-style run tied R3 (0.1227 vs 0.1221, 144 rows gained against 139 lost). Given that Instruments is where VRec's published margin is thinnest, that tie is consistent with the paper rather than a failed reproduction. Your 150-cluster verifier lost (0.1160), which suggests the published k range of 15–25 was chosen for a reason the paper never states.

## Every published retriever-training loss only ranks evidence it already retrieved

Retriever training from generator feedback comes in two families. **Marginal-likelihood methods** treat the document as a latent variable and maximise log Σ_{z∈top-k} p(z|x)p(y|x,z): REALM, RAG and EMDR2. **Distillation methods** build a target distribution over the retrieved candidates from the generator's own signal and minimise KL to the retriever's softmax. The target signal can be cross-attention (FiD-distill), the likelihood of y given each document (Atlas PDist, RePlug LSR), or the loss increase when a document is left out (Atlas LOOP). In Atlas's controlled comparison at 64-shot NQ, a fixed retriever scores **39.9**, ADist 40.9, EMDR2 43.3, **PDist 45.0** and LOOP 41.8. The authors find "no systematic winner" and choose PDist for stability and cost ([Atlas, arXiv 2208.03299](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.03299)). RAG gains 2.8–9.4 points from learning the query encoder rather than freezing it, for example NQ EM 37.8→43.5 for RAG-Token ([arXiv 2005.11401](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). RePlug reports +6.3% on GPT-3 language modelling with a black-box LM ([arXiv 2301.12652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12652)). Its exact LSR equation and temperature values are **unverified, from memory**. So are the REALM, FiD-distillation and EMDR2 formulations ([REALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909), [FiD-distill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04584), [EMDR2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05346)), although Atlas's restatements of PDist, EMDR2 and ADist were checked.

These losses share one structural property that bears directly on your **23% candidate recall on R3's misses**. In Atlas, the retriever distribution is normalised over the top-K only, so these objectives cannot teach a retriever to surface evidence it never put in its candidate set ([arXiv 2208.03299](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.03299)). Hindsight measured this ceiling and broke it. Its target-conditioned guide retriever Q(z|x,y) is trained with an ELBO, E_Q[log p(y|x,z)] − KL(Q‖P_prior), and training samples evidence from an α-mixture of prior and posterior that shifts toward the prior over time. On Wizard of Wikipedia, the prior retriever's **Success@5 rose from 49.9 to 58.8**, above the marginal-likelihood retriever's **Success@100 of 55.8**, where that retriever had saturated. The posterior itself reached S@10 of 88.4 ([Hindsight, ICLR 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.07752)). Hindsight's diagnosis also fits your situation. On one-to-many tasks the generator's likelihood is flat across many plausible pieces of evidence, the marginal-likelihood signal weakens, and the retriever settles on "safe" similar evidence. The caveat matters just as much: **end-to-end top-1 F1 barely moved (18.79→18.86)** even as retrieval recall jumped ([arXiv 2110.07752](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.07752)). Expect recall-of-useful-evidence to improve much more than HR@10.

Your setting avoids the expensive parts of this literature. The corpus is small and fixed, with 20 or 150 clusters, so there is no stale index. That problem costs Atlas about 30% overhead for periodic re-indexing ([arXiv 2208.03299](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.03299)). The posterior can also be computed **exactly rather than learned**: Q(c|x,y) ∝ p_dec(y | x, c) over every cluster, using the frozen decoder. This target differs from the label your verifiers were trained on. "Which cluster contains the target" is a containment label. PDist asks "under which evidence does the decoder assign high probability to the target", which is a utility label. A 63% conversion rate means the two labels disagree on a meaningful fraction of rows. Freezing the decoder while training the prior also avoids RAG's documented failure mode, where retrieval "collapses" to input-independent documents and the generator learns to ignore them ([arXiv 2005.11401](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)).

Recommendation-specific evidence for learned retrievers is thinner and partly negative. UBR4CTR alternates training of a CTR predictor and a behaviour-retrieval query generator ([arXiv 2005.14171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14171)). That its retriever uses REINFORCE is **unverified**. ETA makes long-sequence retrieval end-to-end through LSH over shared learned embeddings ([arXiv 2108.04468](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.04468)). In LLM sequential recommendation, one demonstration-selection study found a **trained contrastive demo retriever less robust than plain LLM-embedding similarity** ([arXiv 2403.10135](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.10135)). That result mirrors your label-trained verifiers and points the same way: the supervision target, not model capacity, is what is wrong. The notes found no recommendation paper that applies Atlas/RePlug-style KL-to-decoder-posterior training, or a Hindsight posterior, to CF evidence for an LLM recommender.

## Coverage outruns selection, and likelihood fails as a selector

In general LLM reasoning, the gap between having a correct path and picking it is well documented. In Large Language Monkeys, coverage on MATH rises from **79.8% to 95.3%** as samples go from 100 to 10,000, while majority voting and reward-model selection stay at **38.7%→39.8%**. Where an exact verifier exists (unit tests on SWE-bench Lite), coverage converts directly into accuracy, from 15.9% to 56% ([arXiv 2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787v3)). Your oracle-vs-R3 gap (0.2275 vs 0.1450) is the recommendation version of this, and no recsys paper reports a comparable oracle-vs-selector analysis.

What closes this gap in the literature falls into three groups, ordered by how well each transfers to your setup.

**Marginalization over paths** needs no training. Self-consistency votes over final answers and lifts PaLM-540B on GSM8K from **56.5% to 74.4%** ([arXiv 2203.11171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11171v2)).

**Trained outcome verifiers** are the second group. A 6B verifier roughly matches a 30× larger model ([arXiv 2110.14168](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14168)). Generative verifiers scored by P("Yes") raise GSM8K best-of-N from **73% to 93.4%** ([GenRM, ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/214308a2d5e3f83ef9ad2739e1cbc46d-Abstract-Conference.html)). Process reward models solve 78% of a representative MATH subset ([arXiv 2305.20050](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050)). In recommendation, a post-hoc generative verifier that scores identifier tokens given the retriever's query state improves Recall@10 for SASRec, GRU4Rec and others. The abstract gives no numbers ([arXiv 2609.12270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12270)).

**Scores corrected for input-independent priors** are the third group. Domain-conditional PMI, log P(y|x) − log P(y|domain), beats raw likelihood consistently because it removes "surface-form competition" and a-priori-likely answers ([Holtzman et al., EMNLP 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08315)). The related tools are contrastive decoding, MMI reranking, calibrate-before-use and semantic entropy. All of these are **unverified, from memory**.

Your below-chance decoder log-prob selector (7.3% vs 13%) fits the PMI diagnosis. Each path is scored by P(items | user, cluster c). A decoder trained to trust its conditioning finds every path plausible *given its own cluster*, so comparing those scores across clusters compares quantities with different implicit priors. Generic, popular-cluster paths look most confident and win. The model-error literature makes the same point for MT, where the exact mode is often degenerate. One version, that the mode is often the empty translation, is **unverified, from memory** ([Stahlberg & Byrne, EMNLP 2019](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1331.xml)). The principled correction is a noisy-channel product: log P(c|x) + log P(items|x,c) − log P(items|c). Your cluster predictor (about 23% on misses) supplies the first term. A user-agnostic pass under the same cluster supplies the third. The notes found no recommendation paper that applies PMI or contrastive scoring to choose among generated hypotheses.

The recommendation-specific evidence on routing is the strongest warning against continuing to build hard selectors. RouteRec routes per request among four recommenders plus an LLM reranker on MovieLens-1M. Under a leakage-free protocol, **hard selection reached HR@10 0.223, below BM25's 0.254, while the oracle was 0.584**. **Gated item-level aggregation of candidate lists reached 0.295**. The authors attribute the failure to sparse switching labels, with one held-out target per user ([arXiv 2607.09908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09908)). Calibration and deferral work offers the right formal frame. You have 8 cluster-conditioned "experts" plus R3 as the default, and learning-to-defer trains a rejector with a consistent cost-sensitive surrogate from samples of each expert's correctness ([Mozannar & Sontag, ICML 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/mozannar20b.html)). VRec's entropy maximisation on negatives is a selective-prediction signal of the same kind ([arXiv 2603.07725](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07725v1)). LLM self-evaluation is calibrated only "in the right format" and degrades on new tasks ([Kadavath et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.05221)), which is why selectors are external. RouteRec's lesson is still that **with one target per row, the deferral classifier is data-starved**. Soft aggregation sidesteps the problem rather than solving it. Recsys precedent for "aggregate, don't select" includes multi-interest models such as ComiRec, which merge items across interest vectors (**unverified, from memory**). Parallel Latent Reasoning aggregates several latent streams, but its claim of more than 5% gains comes from a secondary summary only ([arXiv 2601.03153](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03153)).

## Neighbour memories are strong baselines, but only new inputs fix shared misses

Your corpus of CF clusters and other samples' latents is a latent memory of the kind used by kNN-LM, RaSeRec and RIM. kNN-LM interpolates λ·p_kNN + (1−λ)·p_LM over a datastore of (frozen hidden state → next token) with **no retriever training**, and its gains are largest on rare patterns. This is **unverified, from memory**, as is the follow-up attributing the gains to key-representation choice and temperature ([kNN-LM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00172); [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02828)). TRIME instead shapes the representation with in-batch memories during training (**unverified, from memory**; [arXiv 2205.12674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12674)). In recommendation, RaSeRec stores latent user representations *paired with their next-item targets* and reads them by dual-channel cross-attention over a SASRec backbone ([arXiv 2412.18378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18378)). RIM retrieves other training rows *with their labels* via a BM25-style engine and beats prior methods across 11 datasets ([arXiv 2108.05252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05252)). Neighbourhood heuristics also stay strong: across 12 session-based algorithms, simple nearest-neighbour schemes were preferable to the neural ones "in most cases" ([Ludewig et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09587)). For latent memories, the lever the literature identifies is the **key space**, not a separately learned retriever. One untried variant for you is to key the memory by the decoder's own hidden state at the thought slot and to use the neighbours' *targets* as values. Still, the notes found no paper that retrieves other users' **latent thought vectors** or reasoning traces as evidence. That is an open niche.

Complementarity is where the literature is most sobering. Classic CF boosting reweights training samples across rounds ([ACML 2013](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v29/Jiang13.html); BoostFM at [IUI 2017](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/135914)). Diversity ensembles such as EMKD combine parallel encoders ([arXiv 2304.14668](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.14668)). CVaR-DRO on SASRec up-weights the highest-loss examples in each batch ([arXiv 2501.18117](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18117v1)). All of these report aggregate or worst-group gains. **The notes found no paper that trains a second recommender on a base model's misses and reports results on those misses**, and that absence is itself informative. Each of these methods reweights the *same* history-derived signal. When every history-based retriever fails on the same rows, as in your setup, there is nothing new to reweight toward. RouteRec's aggregation only helped because its agents' candidate unions differed, holding the target for **50.8–58.4%** of users ([arXiv 2607.09908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09908)). Complementarity has to come from a different *input*.

Two input sources have documented per-group gains on the rows where ID/history models are weak. **LLM-embedded neighbour users plus semantic item views** (LLM-ESR, NeurIPS 2024) raise SASRec on Beauty from **H@10 0.0870 to 0.2257 for tail items and 0.4270 to 0.5581 for tail users**. These are sampled-candidate metrics, so the absolute values are inflated. Its Fashion dataset, with an average sequence length of 3.82 and about 4.7K items, is the closest public analogue to your Instruments data with a mean length of 4.4 ([arXiv 2405.20646](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20646)). The key detail: LLM-ESR finds neighbours by **LLM text embeddings of the history**, a different similarity space from your history-ID retrievers, so it could reach different rows. Whether it fixes the *same* misses has never been measured. **Item text on Amazon Instruments** (Recformer) lifts cold-item NDCG@10 from **0.0142 (SASRec) to 0.0315**, but cold-item accuracy stays about 3× below in-set accuracy ([arXiv 2305.13731](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.13731)). KAR-style LLM knowledge augmentation gives about 1–1.5% AUC across CTR models with no hard-user breakdown ([arXiv 2306.10933](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10933)). Cross-domain transfer needs overlapping users ([arXiv 2410.13588](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588v1)). With your roughly 1% overlap, only noisy LLM-generated pseudo-items remain ([LLMCDSR](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/llmcdsr-enhancing-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation-with-lar/)).

Google's LLM interest-exploration design is structurally the closest match to your "right coarse group, wrong item" problem. An LLM proposes a novel interest cluster, and a sequential model is *constrained to return items inside it* ([arXiv 2405.16363](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16363v2)). It reports live-traffic gains in exploration, not offline accuracy on misses. Your 20-group verifier is right on 18% of R3's misses, yet those rarely become hits. A 20-way group probably contains hundreds of items, so the group alone does not narrow the target enough for the decoder to land a top-10 hit. Hard constraint plus within-group ranking is the published pattern for converting a correct group into an item.

There may also be a hard ceiling. A 2026 study shows Amazon benchmarks are largely "shortcut-solvable": a few-hop transition graph from the last 1–2 items, ranked by feature similarity, beats the best baselines by **+38–44% NDCG@10** on Sports and CDs ([arXiv 2605.07125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07125)). A search snippet attributes 1-hop transition coverage of test targets of only **about 9% on Beauty and CDs** to this paper. This is **unverified in the full text**. Amazon 5-core targets are almost never repeats, a point the notes flag as background and unverified, so most are "explore" targets, which are much harder ([Li et al., SIGIR 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3539618.3591914)). If your misses are concentrated among targets outside the local transition neighbourhood, no history-based evidence can reach them. That would explain why all your retrievers fail on the same rows.

## Five testable directions, ranked by expected payoff per GPU-hour

The ranking puts cheap, inference-only tests of the "aggregate, don't select" thesis first. Training changes motivated by the retriever-training literature come next. The bet on new information comes last because it is the most expensive. Each direction names a measurement that would refute it as well as one that would confirm it. All comparisons should use the same rows and report gained/lost counts against R3, as your VRec-style run did.

Directions 1, 2 and 4 can run on the same cached paths in the same week. Together they distinguish three explanations for the oracle gap. If it is recoverable by soft combination, Direction 1 shows it. If likelihood is misusing the cluster prior, Direction 2 shows it. If correct groups are too coarse to land an item, Direction 4 shows it. Direction 3 is the principled training-side fix. It is also the one the literature most clearly predicts will raise evidence recall more than HR@10, so it is worth doing only if Direction 1 shows that aggregation can turn better recall into hits. Direction 5's audit is cheap enough to run first as a gate: if most misses have zero co-occurrence coverage, Directions 1–4 are bounded by your oracle rows, and the remaining headroom has to come from new information.

## Conclusion

The reframing p(y|x) = Σ_e p(e|x)p(y|x,e) has already done its job. It separated a working decoder from a failing p(e|x), and the literature explains why every attempt to sharpen p(e|x) with the same supervision and the same inputs has stalled. Hard selection is data-starved with one target per row (RouteRec). Containment labels are not utility labels (Atlas, Hindsight). Self-likelihood is confounded by the evidence it conditions on (PMI). The practical move is to stop treating the sum over evidence as a max: estimate the prior against the decoder's own posterior, and treat *new inputs*, not new retrievers, as the only route to rows that every history-based model misses.

The project is in a position to contribute results the field lacks. The notes found none of the following in any published work. First, a counterfactual probe showing that a latent recommender's decoder uses injected evidence (your 63% conversion and oracle 0.2275). Second, an oracle-vs-selector gap analysis for recommendation reasoning paths. Third, Atlas/Hindsight-style decoder-posterior training of a CF-evidence retriever for an LLM recommender. Fourth, a sweep over the number of verifier groups, which VRec omits and your 20-vs-150 result begins to provide. Fifth, retrieval of other samples' *latent thoughts* as evidence. Sixth, any evaluation of new information sources conditional on a strong base model's misses rather than on popularity-defined tail groups. Even negative outcomes on these six would be publishable, because each one tests an assumption that current latent-reasoning recommenders make without checking.
